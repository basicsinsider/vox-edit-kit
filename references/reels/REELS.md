## typo
Zoom into this text. Notice the subtle glow and softness around the edges. These tiny imperfections are what can make plain text feel like a movie title. Once you know about them, you'll start to notice it everywhere. Before digital filmmaking, titles were often photographed on a film, introducing subtle imperfections that gave them their signature look. First is Halation, a reddish glow around bright edges. Recreate this with inner and outer glow. Next up is film grain. Just add a layer of animated grain on top, and you can use the add grain effect. And finally, there was some softness from the optical process, which we can create with Gauchan Blur at a low setting. Made a free preset that does all this for you and After Effects and Premier. ## news
Look at today's New York Times. See it? Now zoom in really close. You'll see it's not actually a smooth image. It's a grid of dots. And they're the secret sauce to why some images feel alive. Take them away. It feels sterile, like a plain PDF file. So here's my process. They're called half tones, an artifact of old screen printing methods. Because printing presses can't print different shades of black, they convert shades into dot patterns. Dark is bigger, denser dots. Light is smaller, spaced out dots. Here's how we apply them to your work. First grab a half tone texture. Texture Labs is my go-to. Set it to overlay or linear light, and use curves to dial in the strength. If the dots are too big, just scale them down using CC Reptile to extend the pattern without losing your edges. Now your work is tactile and printed, just like the New York Times.

## texture
Texturing is what makes great design feel real. And picking the right texture is important, but how you apply it is even more important. Let's talk blend modes. Here's the easiest way to think about them. If your texture has white background, use the darkened blend modes. They'll ignore the white and keep the darker details. If your texture has a black background, use the lightened blend modes. They'll ignore the black and keep the brighter details. But if you're using any of these ones like overlay or soft light, you need to do something called normalization first. First to saturate your colors, and then you need to apply a levels effect. You're then gonna bring this middle gray point into the middle of the histogram's peak. That centers the texture around 50% gray, so the blend mode adds texture without shifting the colors underneath. Look how much cleaner that is. And then if you decide you want it a little bit stronger, you can only stack another levels effect on top and dial it into your taste.

## streaks
Look at this title sequence. See it? Notice those subtle streaks of light moving behind the text. They give the animation this dramatic premium look. Take them away and suddenly it feels way less alive. The secret is an effect and after effective fractal noise. And by itself, it looks pretty stupid. It's basically just random gray noise. But it's actually the starting point for a ton of really unique effects. Here's how you can create this background with it. First create a solid layer and add fractal noise. Then under transform, turn off uniform scaling and compress the scale width until you get these thin streaks of light. Then animate the evolution with a simple time times 200 expression to keep those streaks constantly shifting. After that, drop on a levels effect to crush the contrast, then just layer in some texture, and you're left with this really cinematic, premium looking title sequence.

## collage
Collage style animation is all about cohesion. Skip these two steps and your composition falls apart fast. First build a color system. It kind of feels like cheating, but when I'm picking colors, I usually just color pick from a few strong references. To make your photos fit this color system, use the tint effect to map your whites to one color and your blacks to another. You can always dial the tint back for a more subtle look. Second, hide low quality assets with high quality texture. A half tone or fine green can help disguise low quality images and give them a more printed feel. For this half tone, I used a texture I found online and applied my photo to it as a luma matte. Then I tweaked the levels until it felt right. ## oldfootage
Look at this Apollo 11 photo. Look closer. Notice that tiny movement, turn it off, and suddenly it looks like a static JPEG. That subtle movement has been hiding in movies for over a hundred years. I'm gonna show you how to recreate it. Back when footage was shot on film, each frame never landed in exactly the same position inside the camera. That tiny bit of movement called gateweave, combined with a little film flicker, became part of the look we now associate with old footage and documentaries. To recreate it, add a transform effect and apply a subtle wiggle expression to the position. Then add an exposure effect and add a tiny wiggle expression to the exposure. That'll create that flicker. Or if you don't want to build it yourself, I made it into a free drag and drop preset and grab it in my bio.

## imgtrans
Watch this box cut. See how seamless that was? Now watch this one. I felt jarring. I call it cutting the curve, and it's one of the easiest ways to make transitions feel invisible. Here's how to do it. Take your first image and animate the scale with strong easing. Then parent your second image to the first so they share the same exact movement. Now find the point where your animation is moving the fastest. Right there. That's where you make the cut. For box style videos, add posterized time on top and set to 12 frames per second to get that organic choppy feel.
